The vertical rule, cut three ways

All three keep the idea from the last round — a mark on the left edge instead of a frame around the card — and fix the thing that made it read cheap. Every pick sits beside an ordinary card, because that is the only honest way to judge one.

It was never the stripe. It was the gradient.

The earlier version used three stops — pale gold at the top, full gold in the middle, dark bronze at the bottom. That is the exact recipe for simulated shine, and a surface that appears to catch light reads as plated plastic: prize ribbon, raffle booth, employee of the month.

Real brass and real foil stamping are flat. They get their richness from being dark and low in chroma, not from glinting. So every rule below is a single flat colour, pulled down out of the yellows into the browns.

The second fix is containment. A stripe that runs off the top and bottom of a card looks stuck on; the same stripe clipped to the card’s corner radius, or inset and cut to a measured height, looks built in. Both moves are below.

Deliberately unchanged from the live card: the teal View Roles button stays the only saturated element, the paper carries no wash, and no rule is thickened past the point where it stops reading as a rule.

H

Ledger Rule

One flat rule in deep brass, running the full height and clipped to the card’s corner radius so it curves with the card rather than sitting on top of it. That clip is the whole difference between a printed rule and a sticker — it makes the mark a property of the card, which is precisely how the rejected clapper rail failed.

Brass Inlay

The rule pulls inside the card — inset from the edge, square-ended, and cut to exactly the height of the project-type tile beside it. Nothing runs off an edge, so it reads as an inlaid strip set into a surface rather than a highlight passing through. The card border warms one step to tie the two together.

What each cut costs

Measured in the browser at an 860px viewport, not estimated. A rule takes width from the text column, and a picked card therefore has slightly less room for its title than an ordinary one — the number that matters is the gap between the two, because a pick should never make a card worse than not being picked.

One more thing about J. A phone makes the card tall, so a full-height 26px spine becomes a large block of brass — considerably more colour than it carries on desktop. It still looks handsome there, but it stops being the quiet option. H and I are unaffected at 4px and 3px.

Verdict — H to ship and forget; J if the Pick should be something people can name.

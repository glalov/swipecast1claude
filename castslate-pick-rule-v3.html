Three rules that are not stripes

The first pass varied one idea — a bar on the left edge — by weight and colour. These three change the device instead. A double rule, a bracket, and a ruled margin are three different typographic objects, and each says something a bar cannot.

Why a bar is the weakest of the four options

A solid vertical bar is a graphic mark. It has no meaning of its own, so all it can do is be more or less loud — which is exactly why the first attempt drifted toward shine, and why the fix was to take the shine away. There is no third move available to a bar. You can make it darker or thinner and that is the whole vocabulary.

The three below are typographic marks, and each already means something before CastSlate uses it. A double rule means this is the top of something. A bracket means read this part. A margin means this page is ruled. Borrowed meaning is what makes a mark read as professional rather than applied: the reader has met all three before, in books, and knows what they are for without being told.

Constraints carried over unchanged: flat colour only, no gradient anywhere, no motion, the teal View Roles button stays the only saturated element, and the paper carries no wash.

K

Double Rule

Two brass hairlines with a hairline of paper between them, running the full height and clipped to the card’s radius. A double rule is a typesetter’s device, not a graphic one — it has separated title pages from text blocks since the eighteenth century, and it survives because doubling is unmistakably deliberate. A single line can always be mistaken for a border, a rendering artefact, a mistake. Two lines never can. This is the quietest possible way to be certain.

Closest reference — the double rule under a masthead, or on a share certificate

L

The Bracket

Not a line down the card but a bracket around its content — a hairline that turns fourteen pixels inward at the top and bottom. The difference is grammatical. A stripe sits beside a card; a bracket encloses something, and enclosure is how print has always marked a passage worth reading. It is also the only cut here with no filled area at all, which is why it stays quiet at any width.

M

Margin Rule

The rule stops being decoration on the card and becomes part of its grid: a narrow ruled margin down the left, with the content starting after it. The channel is empty on purpose — that is what a margin is. Because the layout now respects the rule instead of carrying it, this is the only cut that could never be mistaken for something applied afterwards. It is also the most expensive in width, so read the table at the end before choosing it.

What each cut costs

Measured in the browser, not estimated. A rule takes width from the text column, so a picked card has less room for its title than an ordinary one. The gap between the two columns is the number that matters — a pick must never make a card worse than not being picked.
